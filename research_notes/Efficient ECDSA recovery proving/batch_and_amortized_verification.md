# Batch and amortized / sublinear verification of ECDSA signatures and secp256k1 key recovery

Scope: classical batch verification (random linear combination / small exponents test, Naccache et al. 1994 and follow-ups, Antipa et al. 2005, Cheon–Yi 2007, Karati–Das 2012–2014, Bernstein–Doumen–Lange–Oosterwijk 2012), Pippenger/bucket MSM cost models, and how these ideas are used inside SNARKs/zkVMs (circom/halo2/Spartan ECDSA circuits, Nova-style folding for repeated ecrecover, zkVM precompile costs, hinted scalar multiplication). secp256k1/ECDSA focus. Primary sources were read directly where possible (PDFs of BGR98, BDLO12, Karati et al. JCEN 2014, Ed25519 paper, Struik/Certicom slides, vApps/SP1 profiling paper, El Housni Latincrypt 2025 slides, circom batch-ecdsa source).

Notation used throughout: n = batch size, q = secp256k1 group order (256 bits), signature (r_i, s_i) on hash z_i under key Q_i, R_i the nonce point with x(R_i) mod q = r_i, c_i the random batching coefficients of l bits, "ops" = elliptic-curve group operations (additions and doublings counted equally unless stated).

## KQ0. Why ECDSA is not naturally batchable, and the classical literature timeline

### Takeaway
Random-linear-combination batch verification (Naccache–M'Raïhi–Vaudenay–Raphaeli 1994, formalised by Bellare–Garay–Rabin 1998) needs the full nonce point R_i, but a standard ECDSA signature transmits only r_i = x(R_i) mod q, so a verifier must recover ±y(R_i) (a square root and a 2^n sign ambiguity). Every "standard ECDSA" batching paper (Karati–Das et al. 2012–2014) is a fight against this ambiguity and is limited to batches of ≤8; once R_i is known (ECDSA* of Antipa et al. 2005, or the ecrecover setting where the recovery id fixes R), the problem collapses to a single (2n+1)-point multi-scalar multiplication whose cost per signature falls like 1/log n.

### Cited Findings
- Naccache, M'Raïhi, Vaudenay, Raphaeli, "Can D.S.A. be improved? Complexity trade-offs with the digital signature standard", EUROCRYPT 1994, introduced batch verification of DSA by verifying a random linear combination of the signature equations; the interactive variant was broken by Lim and Lee ("Security of interactive DSA batch verification", Electronics Letters 30(19), 1994): either signer or verifier can produce bogus signatures passing the batch test — [Boyd–Pavlovski ASIACRYPT 2000 (citing both)](https://rd.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/3-540-44448-3_5.pdf); [Lim–Lee record](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/security-of-interactive-dsa-batch-verification/812602946557575173-4502)
- Bernstein et al. on NMVR94: "This proposal is not directly applicable to ElGamal, DSA, Schnorr, ECDSA, etc., because all of those systems require computing linear functions (to compute R) rather than merely verifying linear functions; but if R is transmitted instead of H(···), as suggested in [58], then this problem disappears." NMVR94 Table 1 reported "29n" multiplications for n same-signer signatures at a 2^20 security level; adapted to ECDSA at 2^128 it "would require nearly 200 elliptic-curve additions for each signature from the same signer" — [Ed25519 paper, Bernstein–Duif–Lange–Schwabe–Yang 2011, §5 (PDF)](https://ed25519.cr.yp.to/ed25519-20110926.pdf)
- Bellare–Garay–Rabin (EUROCRYPT 1998) proposed the Random Subset, Small Exponents and Bucket tests; BGR reported "3200 multiplications to verify 100 exponentiations, or 6480 multiplications to verify 100 DSA signatures, in both cases at a substandard 2^60 security level" (Ed25519 authors' characterisation of BGR Appendix A.1) — [Ed25519 paper](https://ed25519.cr.yp.to/ed25519-20110926.pdf); [BGR98 PDF](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~mihir/papers/batch.pdf)
- Boyd–Pavlovski, "Attacking and Repairing Batch Verification Schemes", ASIACRYPT 2000: a weakness "applies to almost all known batch verifiers for discrete logarithm based signature schemes"; the repair requires a prime-order group (rules out Z*_n for composite n) — [Springer PDF](https://rd.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/3-540-44448-3_5.pdf); [IACR cryptodb](https://www.iacr.org/cryptodb/data/paper.php?pubkey=156)
- Antipa, Brown, Gallant, Lambert, Struik, Vanstone, "Accelerated verification of ECDSA signatures", SAC 2005 (LNCS 3897, pp. 307–318): cuts single ECDSA verification time by >40% with little added complexity; ECDSA* (transmit R instead of r) is introduced there; the technique is patented by Certicom (US 10,284,370 granted 2019, priority Jan 2005) — [DBLP](https://dblp.org/rec/conf/sacrypt/AntipaBGLSV05); [USPTO](https://uspto.report/patent/grant/10284370); [Struik slides (Fields Institute)](https://av.fields.utoronto.ca/programs/scientific/07-08/cryptography/rene_struik.pdf)
- Cheon–Yi, "Fast Batch Verification of Multiple Signatures", PKC 2007: uniform sampling of width-w NAF ("sparse") exponents as randomizers; "asymptotically seven and four times as fast" as individual verification for single/multiple signers; for modified ECDSA (R transmitted) from a single signer, "asymptotically only six elliptic curve additions per each signature"; also τ-adic w-NAFs for Koblitz curves. The scheme requires the modified (R-transmitting) ECDSA — [Springer Professional abstract](https://www.springerprofessional.de/fast-batch-verification-of-multiple-signatures/2781684); [IACR archive PDF (403 for automated fetch)](https://www.iacr.org/archive/pkc2007/44500442/44500442.pdf)
- Karati, Das, Roychowdhury, Bellur, Bhattacharya, Iyer, "Batch Verification of ECDSA Signatures", AFRICACRYPT 2012 (LNCS 7374, pp. 1–18), and journal version "New algorithms for batch verification of standard ECDSA signatures", JCEN 2014: "first reported study on batch verification of original ECDSA signatures"; naive algorithm N takes square roots in F_p and must try sign combinations; symbolic algorithms S1/S2 compute Σ R_i symbolically in the unknown y_i; "These algorithms are practical only for small (⩽ 8) batch sizes"; maximum speedup "above six" (same signer) and "about two" (different signers) on NIST prime curves — [JCEN PDF](https://cse.iitkgp.ac.in/~abhij/publications/ECDSA-JCEN.pdf); [Springer Professional](https://www.springerprofessional.de/batch-verification-of-ecdsa-signatures/3918282)
- Karati et al. JCEN 2014, Table 5: "The maximum speedup achieved by our fully ECDSA-compliant algorithms is 6.20 in the case of same signer, and 1.70 in the case of different signers. Both these records are achieved by Algorithm S2′ for the curve P-521 and for the batch size t=7." Ideal speedups are t (same signer) and 2t/(t+1) (different signers) — [JCEN PDF](https://cse.iitkgp.ac.in/~abhij/publications/ECDSA-JCEN.pdf)
- Karati et al. 2014 ACNS follow-up ("Faster batch verification of standard ECDSA signatures using summation polynomials") raised the optimal batch size from 7 to 9 — [Springer Professional](https://www.springerprofessional.de/en/faster-batch-verification-of-standard-ecdsa-signatures-using-sum/2159720)
- Bernstein, Doumen, Lange, Oosterwijk, "Faster batch forgery identification", INDOCRYPT 2012 (ePrint 2012/549), §2 breaks the un-randomised Karati et al. AFRICACRYPT scheme: (1) submit (r,s) and (r,−s) on the same message/key: R and −R cancel; (2) with one known secret key a2, forge a signature under any target key A1: pick k1, R1=k1B, random s1, R2=(r1/s1)A1, s2=(H(M2)+r2 a2)/(k1−H(M1)/s1); both pass the batch; "The second attack would work even if the ECDSA signature system were replaced by a signature system such as EdDSA that transmits R" — the fix is per-signature randomizers z_i — [BDLO PDF (cr.yp.to/badbatch)](https://cr.yp.to/badbatch/badbatch-20120919.pdf); [ePrint 2012/549](https://eprint.iacr.org/2012/549)
- Karati–Das–Roychowdhury, "Using Randomizers for Batch Verification of ECDSA Signatures", ePrint 2012/582: randomizers "can be easily embedded in these symbolic-computation algorithms"; "The performance degradation caused by randomizers is comparable with that associated with ECDSA*" — [ePrint 2012/582](https://eprint.iacr.org/2012/582)
- Effect of randomizers on Karati et al. S2′ (same signer, Table 6): P-256 speedup drops from 5.33 (t=7, no randomizers) to 1.77 with 128-bit randomizers; P-521 from 6.06 to 2.95 (l=128) and 1.93 (l=256); "the case of multiple signers too is severely affected by randomization. Therefore, we do not present experimental data for these cases." — [JCEN PDF](https://cse.iitkgp.ac.in/~abhij/publications/ECDSA-JCEN.pdf)
- 2026 revisit: Paksoy, ElSheikh, Hasan (Waterloo), Cenk (Ripple), "Batch Verification of Modified ECDSA Signatures", ePrint 2026/663: first public C implementations (on libsecp256k1 / OpenSSL) of ECDSA_ast and ECDSA_rec (variants that embed extra bits so R is recoverable); with HSS-rand randomizers and batch 32 on secp256k1 the improvement over individual standard-ECDSA verification is only 30.9% (ECDSA_ast) / 17.2% (ECDSA_rec); on secp256r1 53.9% / 40.5% — [ePrint 2026/663](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/663)

### Inferences
- In the ecrecover setting the ±y ambiguity does not exist: Ethereum signatures carry the recovery id v (parity of y(R) and the rare x overflow), so R_i is fully determined by (r_i, v_i); that is exactly the ECDSA* situation of Antipa et al., and all the Karati-style square-root/symbolic machinery is unnecessary. Only on-curve checks of the reconstructed R_i are needed.
- The small secp256k1 numbers in ePrint 2026/663 (17–31% at batch 32) versus the Θ(log n) asymptotics are consistent with libsecp256k1's individual verification already using GLV + large precomputed G tables, so the "baseline" is already ~2x faster than textbook double-scalar multiplication; batch MSM gains are relative to that strong baseline.

### Gaps
- Full texts of NMVR94, Antipa et al. SAC 2005 and Cheon–Yi PKC 2007 were paywalled/blocked; cost tables are taken from abstracts, Struik's slides and BDLO/Ed25519 citations.
- No per-batch-size breakdown for ePrint 2026/663 beyond batch 32 was available from the abstract.

## KQ1. Cheapest known verification of n signatures with witnessed Q_i and known R_i: the (2n+1)-point MSM and its Pippenger cost as a function of n

### Takeaway
With R_i known and Q_i supplied, the cheapest known check is a single random linear combination Σ_i c_i·(s_i R_i − z_i G − r_i Q_i) = O (equivalently, after scaling by s_i^{-1}, Σ c_i R_i − Σ (c_i r_i/s_i) Q_i − (Σ c_i z_i/s_i) G = O so that the n R_i carry only l-bit scalars), evaluated as one (2n+1)-point MSM with Pippenger's bucket method (or Bos–Coster). Group operations per signature fall from ≈360–384 (individual, JSF/Straus) to ≈96 at n=100 and ≈56 at n=1000 (≈41 at n=10,000) by the standard cost model; measured Bos–Coster code already reached 70 additions/signature at n=64 in 2012.

### Cited Findings
- Batch equation and scalar layout (Ed25519 paper, directly applicable to any linear equation with known R): "We choose independent uniform random 128-bit integers z_i ... and verify the equation (−Σ z_i S_i mod ℓ)B + Σ z_i R_i + Σ (z_i H_i mod ℓ)A_i = 0 by a multi-scalar multiplication ... The number of scalars here is 2n+1. Half of the scalars are 253-bit and half are 128-bit." The authors use Bos–Coster because it needs less storage than Pippenger — [Ed25519 paper §5](https://ed25519.cr.yp.to/ed25519-20110926.pdf)
- Measured batch cost (Ed25519, Nehalem/Westmere): single verification 273,364 cycles; batch of 64: 8.55M cycles = under 134,000 cycles/signature; batch 128: under 125,000; larger batches (L2-resident): under 114,000; ≈44,000 of those cycles are point decompression, so uncompressed inputs would give ≈81,000 cycles/signature at batch 64 — [Ed25519 paper](https://ed25519.cr.yp.to/ed25519-20110926.pdf)
- BDLO 2012 cost model at security level 2^b (scalars 2b bits, randomizers b bits), counting additions: separate verification with Joint Sparse Form costs 3nb (2b doublings + b additions per signature), i.e. 384 ops/signature at b=128; Straus asymptotically (2+8/lg b)nb; their separate3.py (Straus c=4, signed sliding windows) measured "fewer than 2.8nb additions for b=128" (≈358/signature). Batch verification with Straus: total ≈ (2/n + 3/c + 2(2^c−2)/b)·nb, roughly (2/n + 6/lg b)nb; measured straus6.py (b=128, c=5): 1.15nb (n=16), 0.90nb (n=32), 0.86nb (n=64) ≈ 110 additions/signature at n=64; Bos–Coster (boscoster2.py): 0.55nb at n=64 ≈ 70 additions/signature; "Asymptotically the Bos–Coster algorithm costs O(nb/lg n) and Pippenger's algorithm costs O(nb/lg nb)"; Pippenger "is almost twice as fast in some cases, and it is within 1+o(1) of optimal for essentially all sequences of scalars" — [BDLO PDF §5](https://cr.yp.to/badbatch/badbatch-20120919.pdf)
- BDLO on handling the fixed base G separately: with a precomputed table of B, 2B, …, (2^c−1)B and its 2^c-shifts, "Computing any desired multiple of B then takes fewer than 1/c additions for each bit of the scalar, a very small cost compared to the other computations" — [BDLO PDF §4](https://cr.yp.to/badbatch/badbatch-20120919.pdf)
- Pippenger/bucket cost formula (λ-bit scalars, N points, window c): about N + 2^c additions per window, λ/c windows, plus λ doublings; total ≈ (λ/c)(N + 2^c + 1) additions and λ doublings; with c = log2 N this is ≈ 2Nλ/log2 N additions; arkworks uses c = ln N + 2 — [HackMD Pippenger note](https://hackmd.io/@tazAymRSQCGXTUKkbh1BAg/Sk27liTW9); same structure in [curve25519-dalek Pippenger docs](https://doc.servo.org/curve25519_dalek/backend/serial/scalar_mul/pippenger/struct.Pippenger.html) ("cost = (n·A + 2·2^(w/2)·A + w·D + A)·256/w", w = 6..8 chosen from n) and [another note](https://hackmd.io/@sin7y/H1SOBPQ49) giving (b/c)(n + 2^(c−1)) with c = log n → 2bn/log n, "When n ≈ 10^5, the number of group operations reduces to 1/12 of its original value"
- Pippenger bucket-method description used for batch verification (sort points into 2^c buckets per window, discard bucket 0, running-sum reduction S_1 + 2S_2 + … + (2^c−1)S_{2^c−1}) — [BDLO PDF §4](https://cr.yp.to/badbatch/badbatch-20120919.pdf)
- Multi-signer batching caveat from Ed25519 paper: if public keys repeat (NMVR/BGR same-signer setting), the 253-bit scalars on A_i can be merged, which is why they use the equation form that puts the full-size scalar on A_i and the 128-bit scalar on R_i — [Ed25519 paper](https://ed25519.cr.yp.to/ed25519-20110926.pdf)
- In-circuit batch MSM (circom, puma314/batch-ecdsa, 0xPARC summer residency 2022): the batch equation is "sum_i t^i R_i = (sum_i t^i (h_i s_i^{-1})) G + sum_i t^i (r_i s_i^{-1}) Q_i" with the prover supplying rprime = y(R_i); the Secp256k1LinearCombination template uses window w=4 lookup tables per point and shared doublings (Straus-style, not bucket sorting); measured constraints: naive single-sig 1.5M; batch 2: 1.9M; 4: 2.8M; 8: 4.6M; 16: 8.1M; 32: 15.3M (=478k/signature, ≈3.1x better than 1.5M), proving times 4 s / 7 s / 14 s / 40 s / 86 s with rapidsnark on AWS r5.8xlarge; proving key 9.0 GB at batch 32 — [batch-ecdsa README and circuits/batch_ecdsa.circom](https://github.com/puma314/batch-ecdsa); [Succinct blog teaser](https://blog.succinct.xyz/batch-ecdsa/)

### Inferences
- Worked cost model for the ecrecover batch (my computation from the formulas above; signed-digit buckets, 2^c additions per window for reduction, both scalar classes share buckets per window, 256 doublings; n R_i with 128-bit coefficients c_i, n+1 points (Q_i, G) with 256-bit scalars after scaling each equation by c_i/s_i):
  - n = 100, c = 6: bucket insertions 43·101 + 22·100 = 6,543; reductions 43·64 = 2,752; doublings 256 → ≈9,550 ops ≈ 96 ops/signature (c=5 gives ≈9,770, c=7 ≈10,630).
  - n = 1,000, c = 8: 32·1,001 + 16·1,000 = 48,032; reductions 32·256 = 8,192; +256 → ≈56,500 ops ≈ 56 ops/signature (c=7: ≈61,000; c=9: ≈59,100).
  - n = 10,000, c = 11: 24·10,001 + 12·10,000 = 360,024; reductions 24·2,048 = 49,152; +256 → ≈409,000 ops ≈ 41 ops/signature.
  - Compared with ≈358–384 ops for an individual JSF/Straus verification (BDLO's numbers), this is ≈3.7x (n=100), ≈6.4x (n=1,000), ≈9x (n=10,000) fewer group operations. Compared with a GLV-accelerated individual secp256k1 verification (≈128 doublings + ≈100–130 additions, i.e. ≈230–260 ops), the gains are ≈2.5x / 4.3x / 6x. These figures are consistent with BDLO's measured 70 additions/signature (Bos–Coster, n=64) and the Ed25519 batch-64 speed (≈2x in cycles including decompression).
  - If instead all 2n+1 scalars are left at 256 bits (no s_i^{-1} normalisation), n=1,000 costs ≈72 ops/signature; the n batch inversions needed for normalisation cost ≈3n field multiplications + 1 inversion with Montgomery's trick, so normalising is always worth it.
  - Applying the secp256k1 GLV endomorphism inside the batch MSM (each 256-bit scalar → two 128-bit scalars on P and ψ(P)) only helps marginally in the bucket model (≈82 vs 96 ops/signature at n=100; ≈52 vs 56 at n=1,000) because the bucket insertions are already the dominant term and doublings are already amortised.
- The reduction from ≈384 to ≈56 ops/signature is where the batching win lives; the remaining per-signature costs (two modular inversions or a batch inverse, hashing, coefficient derivation, on-curve checks for R_i and Q_i) are field-level and small by comparison.
- Inside a SNARK the same equation is used, but Pippenger's data-dependent bucket sorting is awkward to arithmetise, so existing circuits (circom batch-ecdsa) use Straus-style shared doublings with per-point windows: cost per point ≈ 256/w additions + a 2^w table, independent of n, which caps the in-circuit saving at roughly the ratio (doublings + additions)/(additions) ≈ 3x, matching the measured 1.5M → 478k constraints at batch 32.

### Gaps
- No published secp256k1-specific, cycle-accurate benchmark of batched ecrecover verification with Pippenger at n = 100 / 1,000 was found; the per-signature numbers above are model-derived (and cross-checked against BDLO/Ed25519 measurements on other curves).
- Bernstein's "Pippenger's exponentiation algorithm" PDF (cr.yp.to/papers/pippenger.pdf) was downloaded but its Type-3 font text is not extractable; the formal lower-bound/optimality statements are therefore cited only via BDLO's summary.
- halo2-ecc (Axiom) uses Pippenger in its in-circuit MSM gadget per its README, but its secp256k1 ECDSA benchmark CSV (src/secp256k1/results/ecdsa_bench.csv) could not be retrieved; no halo2 per-signature number is reported here.

## KQ2. Randomness required for soundness of the random linear combination in a non-interactive proof, and bounds for 64-/128-bit coefficients

### Takeaway
In a prime-order group, l-bit uniformly random, per-signature, independent coefficients give soundness error exactly 2^-l for the batch (BGR98 Theorem 3.3; BDLO/Ed25519 restate it as 2^-128 for 128-bit randomizers); the coefficients must be unpredictable to whoever chooses the batch, so in a Fiat–Shamir setting they must be hashed from every value that determines the equation (r_i, s_i, z_i, Q_i and the hinted y(R_i)); an adversary who can re-sample the batch (grinding) multiplies the 2^-l error by its number of hash queries, so 64-bit coefficients buy ≈64-bit security and 128-bit coefficients are the standard choice.

### Cited Findings
- BGR98 Theorem 3.3: "Given a group G of prime order q and a generator g of G. Then Small Exponents Test is a batch verifier for the relation EXP_{G,g}(·,·) with cost l + n(1 + l/2) + ExpCost_G(k1) multiplications, where k1 = |q|" (error 2^-l with l-bit exponents: "setting t = l yields the desired error 2^-l"); Bucket Test cost min_{m≥2} ⌈l/(m−1)⌉·(n + m + 2^{m−1}(m+2) + ExpCost_G(k1)); "for small values of n the Small Exponents Test is better, while for larger values, Bucket Test wins"; "In practice, setting l to be about 60, meaning an error of 2^-60, should suffice" (1998 advice) — [BGR98 PDF](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~mihir/papers/batch.pdf)
- BGR98 §3.5 on non-prime order: "When the order is not prime, the Small Exponents Test (and hence the Bucket Test) do not work; it is easy to find counter-examples" (in Z*_p the instance (x, p−y),(x, y) passes whenever s_1 is even, so error 1/2) — [BGR98 PDF](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~mihir/papers/batch.pdf)
- BDLO 2012: "If the verifier chooses the 'randomizers' z_1, z_2, ... as independent uniform random 128-bit integers then this test cannot be fooled with probability above 2^-128. We emphasize the importance of including these randomizers" — [BDLO PDF §1](https://cr.yp.to/badbatch/badbatch-20120919.pdf)
- Ed25519 paper's one-line proof: the differences P_i = 8R_i + 8H_iA_i − 8S_iB lie in a cyclic group of prime order ℓ; "if P_4 is nonzero then the choices of z_1, z_2, z_3, z_5, z_6, ... determine exactly one choice of z_4 satisfying Σ z_iP_i = 0, and z_4 has chance at most 2^-128 of matching that choice" — [Ed25519 paper §5](https://ed25519.cr.yp.to/ed25519-20110926.pdf)
- BDLO on small randomizers: using "much smaller randomizers, say 20 bits each" at lower tree levels of forgery identification is possible, but "an attacker can fool the smaller randomizers with noticeable probability, on the scale of 2^-20", so results must be re-confirmed against the large-randomizer root — [BDLO PDF §3](https://cr.yp.to/badbatch/badbatch-20120919.pdf)
- Karati et al. 2014 on randomizer length: "for l-bit randomizers, the probability of successful attacks against batch verification is 2^-l"; for a d-bit field, "d/2-bit randomizers do not degrade the security of the ECDSA scheme. Another possibility is to take l = 128 to get 128-bit security independent of the security guarantees of ECDSA" — [JCEN PDF §9](https://cse.iitkgp.ac.in/~abhij/publications/ECDSA-JCEN.pdf)
- Boyd–Pavlovski 2000 attacks apply to "almost all known batch verifiers for discrete logarithm based signature schemes" when the group is not of prime order — [Springer PDF](https://rd.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/3-540-44448-3_5.pdf)
- Production practice: Zcash-ecosystem and FROST batch verifiers (decaf377-rdsa, frost-core, ed25519-zebra) draw a fresh 128-bit random coefficient per signature — [decaf377-rdsa batch.rs](https://docs.rs/decaf377-rdsa/0.11.0/src/decaf377_rdsa/batch.rs.html); [frost-core batch.rs](https://docs.rs/frost-core/2.2.0/src/frost_core/batch.rs.html); [ed25519-zebra batch.rs](https://docs.rs/ed25519-zebra/1.0.1/src/ed25519_zebra/batch.rs.html)
- Fiat–Shamir derivation in existing circuits: circom batch-ecdsa derives a single t = Poseidon-chain over all (r_i, s_i, msghash_i, pubkey_i) limbs and uses coefficients t^i ("Use poseidon hash to get a random t for summations") — [batch_ecdsa.circom](https://github.com/puma314/batch-ecdsa); an optimistic-rollup design note uses the same powers-of-t form with t ∈ [0, n−1] but leaves derivation unspecified — [HackMD batch-ECDSA rollup note](https://hackmd.io/bQXPIvJRRt2-PYg9PBTmFg)
- General Fiat–Shamir soundness analyses for random linear combinations bound the probability that the hash output lands in a "bad challenge" set per query (e.g. the Last Challenge Attack on a batched KZG verifier where "the challenge used to batch the evaluation was derived insufficiently") — [ePrint 2024/994](https://eprint.iacr.org/2024/994); [HackMD Fiat–Shamir RLC note](https://hackmd.io/@adr1anh/BJw1g0aBT)

### Inferences
- Minimal correct recipe for a non-interactive batched ecrecover check inside a proof: (1) on-curve checks for every reconstructed R_i (from r_i, v_i) and every witnessed Q_i (secp256k1 has cofactor 1, so on-curve ⇒ in the prime-order group and BGR's prime-order hypothesis holds); (2) coefficients c_i = H(transcript of all r_i, s_i, z_i, Q_i, v_i/y_i) with the hash absorbing every value before any coefficient is used (BDLO attack 1 exploits exactly a coefficient-independent choice of ±R_i; attack 2 exploits adaptively chosen s_2 — both are excluded only if those values are in the hash preimage); (3) independent l-bit c_i, or powers t^i of one full-field t.
- Grinding bound: an adversary that can try T different batches/witness values (each giving fresh coefficients under the random-oracle model) succeeds with probability ≤ T·2^-l for independent l-bit coefficients. With l = 64 and a 2^40–2^60 online hashing budget the residual security is 4–24 bits short of 64 and far below 128; l = 128 (or full-field) is the choice that matches the 128-bit security of secp256k1. In a zk-proof the grinding is cheap (the prover controls the witness), so there is no reason to go below 128-bit coefficients; the MSM cost difference between 128-bit and 256-bit coefficients on the R_i terms is ≈15–20% of the batch MSM (KQ1 model), and between 64- and 128-bit it is ≈8–10%.
- Powers-of-t coefficients: the batch equation becomes the evaluation of a degree-(n−1) polynomial with group-element coefficients at t; by Schwartz–Zippel the error is ≤ (n−1)/|T| for t drawn from a set T, i.e. ≈ n·2^-l for an l-bit t. For a full 254/256-bit t this is negligible; for a 128-bit t it is 2^-(128−log2 n), acceptable; for 64-bit t it drops to ≈2^-54 at n = 1,000 before grinding. (Derived from the standard polynomial-identity argument; not stated in the sources above.)
- Deriving coefficients from a hash in-circuit costs n Poseidon/other hashes over ≈2n+1 field elements; the circom batch-ecdsa implementation hashes every limb of every input, which is the main non-EC overhead of the approach.

### Gaps
- No source gives a formal, published soundness theorem specifically for "Fiat–Shamir over witnessed values" in the ecrecover setting; the bounds above combine BGR Theorem 3.3 with the standard random-oracle argument.
- The Zcash protocol specification's own batch-validation appendix was not retrieved (only implementations were).

## KQ3. Systems implementing batched ECDSA/ecrecover proving and reported per-signature costs

### Takeaway
Reported per-signature costs span four orders of magnitude depending on the arithmetisation: ≈3k R1CS constraints on the secq256k1 "right field" (Spartan-ecdsa / Nova-based aggregators, with T = r^{-1}R and U = −r^{-1}zG precomputed outside), ≈160k–480k constraints per signature in BN254-field circom (efficient-ecdsa, batch-ecdsa at batch 32), ≈1.5M constraints for naive circom-ecdsa, and ≈48k–75k RISC-V cycles per ecrecover with secp256k1 precompiles in SP1 (2–6% of the cycles of real Ethereum blocks). No production zkEVM/L1 prover was found to use random-linear-combination batching of ecrecover; they pay per signature.

### Cited Findings
- circom-ecdsa (0xPARC, 2022): ECDSA verify 1,508,136 constraints, 45 s proving (Groth16/snarkjs on 16-core c5.4xlarge), 934 MB proving key, witness generation 175 s; pubkey generation 95,444 constraints; the authors warn circuits are "not properly constrained" checked — [circom-ecdsa README](https://github.com/0xPARC/circom-ecdsa)
- efficient-ecdsa (Personae Labs, 2022–2023): verification s·R = m·G + r·Q is rewritten as s·T + U = Q with T = r^{-1}R, U = −r^{-1}mG computed outside the circuit (R is recoverable from T, which "does not reveal the user's public key"); constraints 1,508,136 → 163,239 with precomputed multiples of T (stride 8: (2^8)^i·j·T for i∈[0,31], j∈[0,255]) and 315,175 with Keccak address derivation; original proving "roughly 5 minutes" in-browser — [efficient-ecdsa post (mirror)](https://digital-garden-bpz.pages.dev/blog/efficient-ecdsa); [ethresear.ch thread](https://ethresear.ch/t/efficient-ecdsa-signature-verification-using-circom/13629)
- Spartan-ecdsa (Personae Labs, 2023): 3,039 constraints for efficient-ECDSA verification over secq256k1 (right-field arithmetic, no big-int emulation); standard ECDSA would add 11,494 constraints (wrong-field s^{-1}, r s^{-1}, m s^{-1}) + 3,011 (extra scalar mult) = 14,505; full group-membership circuit 8,076 constraints, 4 s proving in browser / 2 s Node.js on M1 MacBook Pro, 16 kB proof; unaudited; a June 2023 yAcademy audit found a high-severity under-constraining bug — [spartan-ecdsa README](https://github.com/personaelabs/spartan-ecdsa); [audit](https://dev-reports-electisec.netlify.app/reports/06-2023-Spartan-ECDSA)
- circom batch-ecdsa (Succinct/0xPARC residency, 2022): see KQ1 table; batch 32 = 15.3M constraints (478k/signature), 86 s proving on AWS r5.8xlarge — [batch-ecdsa README](https://github.com/puma314/batch-ecdsa)
- Nova aggregation over the secp/secq cycle (dmpierre/nova-browser-ecdsa, 2023): "the efficient ECDSA format devised by Personae" brings cost to "approximately 3k constraints" per signature; 10 signatures per folding step = 30,390 non-linear constraints; the authors estimate "2 to 6 signatures per second in the browser" for 300 signatures — [nova-browser-ecdsa README](https://github.com/dmpierre/nova-browser-ecdsa); [HackMD write-up (403 for automated fetch)](https://hackmd.io/mArMuUx5TC2LEcYecc741Q)
- BEATS (Liu, Wei, Liu, Wang, Hu, Kou, "Scalable batch verification of ECDSA for blockchain using IVC", Frontiers of Computer Science 20(4), 2026, online Feb 2025): Nova over secq256k1/secp256k1 with final SpartanBP compression; ≈4,000 R1CS constraints per ECDSA verification; prover for 2^11 signatures: 248 s (block b=1), 160 s (b=2), 62 s (b=8), 45 s (b=32) vs 776 s for monolithic SpartanBP, i.e. ≈22–121 ms/signature; verifier 87–440 ms vs 21 s; peak memory < 1 GB vs > 10 GB; beats one-by-one native verification on the verifier side only above ≈210 signatures; "3–7x" (abstract) vs "3–17x" (body) prover speedup inconsistency; i7-11800H, 16 GB, bellpepper — [journal page](https://journal.hep.com.cn/fcs/EN/10.1007/s11704-025-41269-5)
- SP1 (zkVM) ecrecover with secp256k1 precompile: average 47,768.3 cycles per ecrecover call (std 7,884) over 600 mainnet blocks, 15.9 cycles/gas (vs modexp 865 cycles/gas); recover_senders is 2.16% / 3.08% / 5.93% of total cycles for three profiled blocks (bn254-heavy, bls-heavy, small 16M-cycle block) — [vApps paper, arXiv 2504.14809, Tables 2 and 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.14809)
- SP1 single-ecrecover microbenchmark (KalqiX team PR, 2025): 74,515 cycles and 783 syscalls per secp256k1 ecrecover; Ed25519 verify 124,975; P-256 ECDSA 151,237; 2,000 ecrecovers = 108.9M cycles (54,446/signature), "22% of the 500M budget", 146.5M "gas" (trace-area metric); their alternative field-native septic-curve Schnorr reaches 5,163 cycles/signature (not ECDSA) — [SP1 PR #2719](https://github.com/succinctlabs/sp1/pull/2719)
- zkVM single-ECDSA proving times (Fenbushi benchmark, Aug 2025, RTX 5090): RISC Zero 1.0 s (GPU, precompiles), SP1 12 s (GPU), OpenVM 14 s (CPU), Jolt 83 s (no precompiles; "lacks lookup-table support for the finite field arithmetic that ECDSA needs") — [Fenbushi benchmark](https://fenbushi.vc/?p=795)
- RISC Zero exposes patched k256 (secp256k1 ECDSA) as an accelerated crate but publishes no ecrecover cycle count on its precompile page — [RISC Zero precompiles](https://dev.risczero.com/api/next/zkvm/precompiles)
- Sei Giga (2025) explored SP1 and a Spartan/efficient-ECDSA circuit for signature verification: zkVM "a few seconds" per single signature was judged "insufficient"; the Spartan route reached ≈200 ms proof verification; batching was not used ("batching can make costs worse unless repeated steps are shared"); the effort was paused after native code optimisations — [Sei blog](https://blog.sei.io/optimizing-ecdsa-signature-verification-exploring-zero-knowledge-proofs-in-the-sei-giga-blockchain/)
- Rollup-side motivation: replacing ≈200 ECDSA signatures in an L1 commitment with one 131-byte SNARK + sender addresses is estimated to cut calldata gas from ≈200K to ≈66K — [HackMD rollup note](https://hackmd.io/bQXPIvJRRt2-PYg9PBTmFg)
- zkEVM precompile handling: Linea, Polygon zkEVM, Scroll and Taiko substitute proof-efficient internal algorithms for precompiles; Linea proves precompiles in separate modules; no per-signature row counts are published in the survey — [arXiv 2510.05376](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.05376)
- ePrint 2024/1914 ("Generic, Fast and Short Proofs for Composite Statements") claimed 2.1 s ECDSA proving, 70x over Groth16, but was withdrawn on 2025-05-16 — [ePrint 2024/1914](https://eprint.iacr.org/2024/1914)
- Mainnet context: ≈70.4M precompile calls and 188.7B gas over ≈426k blocks (≈165 precompile calls/block), with modexp and ecrecover dominating precompile gas — [ethPandaOps precompile study](http://investigations.ethpandaops.io/2026-03/precompile-usage-mainnet/)

### Inferences
- Normalising reported costs to "equivalent group operations per signature": the SP1 precompile path (≈48k–75k cycles, 783 syscalls) corresponds to a full double-scalar multiplication executed as ≈780 point add/double syscalls, i.e. no batching and no GLV/half-scalar trick; a batched MSM at n = 1,000 would need ≈56 group ops per signature (KQ1), a ≈14x reduction in EC syscalls, before accounting for hashing/coefficient costs.
- All "≈3k constraints per signature" numbers rely on (a) right-field arithmetic (secq256k1 scalar field = secp256k1 base field) and (b) moving the fixed-base/hash-dependent work (T, U) outside the circuit; (b) is legitimate for ecrecover because R is public, but it shifts ≈2 scalar multiplications per signature to the (untrusted) verifier-side preprocessing, which must then be re-verified or trusted — in an L1 prover this cost does not disappear, it moves into the host and must be constrained elsewhere.
- No zkEVM or L1 zkVM prover was found that batches ecrecover across a block with a random linear combination; the batching results that exist (circom batch-ecdsa 3x; BEATS folding) are research prototypes.

### Gaps
- No "zkSign"-named project or 2023–2026 "proving thousands of ECDSA signatures" demo with published per-signature numbers was found beyond nova-browser-ecdsa (estimate only), BEATS and the KalqiX SP1 measurement.
- No ecrecover cycle counts were found for RISC Zero, OpenVM, ZisK, Pico or Airbender; Succinct's "Batch ECDSA in zkSNARKs" blog body and Personae's "ZK-ECDSA 2" Notion page were unreachable (404/empty).
- halo2-ecc (Axiom) secp256k1 ECDSA benchmark numbers (k, advice columns, proving time) were not retrievable.

## KQ4. Do folding/accumulation schemes (Nova/HyperNova/ProtoStar/ProtoGalaxy) give an asymptotic or practical win for repeated ecrecover?

### Takeaway
Folding removes the per-step SNARK/FFT overhead and keeps memory constant (BEATS: < 1 GB vs > 10 GB, prover 3–17x faster than monolithic Spartan at 2^11 signatures), but it does not reduce the arithmetic per signature below one in-circuit ECDSA check (≈3–4k R1CS on the secq field); per-signature cost stays constant (≈22–121 ms in BEATS, 2–6 sig/s in-browser for nova-browser-ecdsa). Only Nova-based results exist; no HyperNova/ProtoStar/ProtoGalaxy ECDSA benchmarks were found.

### Cited Findings
- BEATS (FCS 2026/online 2025): Nova over the secq256k1/secp256k1 cycle, b signatures per step, Poseidon hash chain binding each step's inputs; ≈4,000 R1CS/signature; prover 2^11 signatures 45 s (b=32) to 248 s (b=1) vs 776 s SpartanBP; verifier 87–440 ms vs ≈21 s; at 2^20 signatures the verifier is 21–174x faster than one-by-one native verification (3–30x vs a parallel baseline), and verifier time stays nearly flat up to ≈2^14 signatures before hashing dominates; memory < 1 GB — [journal page](https://journal.hep.com.cn/fcs/EN/10.1007/s11704-025-41269-5); [EurekAlert release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1125728)
- nova-browser-ecdsa (2023): 10 signatures/step, 30,390 constraints/step, estimated 2–6 signatures/s in-browser (M2 Pro, circom wasm witness generator, no compressed Spartan proof) — [README](https://github.com/dmpierre/nova-browser-ecdsa)
- Nova-Scotia proof-of-concept for P-256 batch ECDSA folding exists but must emulate P-256 over the BN254/Grumpkin cycle with BigInt arithmetic (no curve cycle for P-256) — [nullity00/batch-ecdsa-p256](https://www.github.com/nullity00/batch-ecdsa-p256)
- ProtoGalaxy's claim: folding k instances at once gives a constant number of verifier field operations per instance (verifier-side claim) — [ProtoGalaxy](https://core.ac.uk/works/152566410)
- Sonobe (PSE/0xPARC) implements Nova and HyperNova/ProtoGalaxy but publishes no ECDSA benchmark; code "not audited", "several optimizations are also pending" — [sonobe](https://sonobe.pse.dev/)

### Inferences
- Asymptotics: per step the Nova prover does O(|C|) group operations (one MSM commitment of the step witness) with no FFT, so cost per signature is Θ(|C_sig|) and independent of n — folding gives constant amortised recursion overhead, not sub-linear signature arithmetic. The KQ1 random-linear-combination trick is what makes per-signature EC work fall like 1/log n; the two are complementary, but combining them is awkward in IVC because the batching coefficients must be derived after all signatures are fixed (requires a two-pass design: first fold a commitment/hash of all inputs, then fold the MSM accumulation with coefficients derived from it).
- Practically, the in-circuit per-signature cost (≈3–4k constraints) is already dominated by a single variable-base scalar multiplication on the right field; a batched in-circuit MSM with Straus-style shared doublings would cut this by at most ≈3x (as in circom batch-ecdsa), so folding-plus-batching could plausibly reach ≈1–1.5k constraints/signature, but no implementation or measurement of this combination was found.

### Gaps
- No HyperNova, ProtoStar or ProtoGalaxy benchmark on ECDSA/ecrecover was found in any source; the Sonobe repository has no such example.
- The nova-browser-ecdsa HackMD benchmark table (actual measured proving time per step) was not accessible (HackMD returned 403 to automated fetches); only the authors' 2–6 sig/s estimate is cited.

## KQ5. Verifying a single scalar multiplication faster than computing it (with or without hints), on secp256k1

### Takeaway
With a hint (the claimed result Q, which is exactly what an ecrecover prover has), verification can be made ≈1.5–2x cheaper than computing: write the scalar as a ratio of half-size integers k ≡ x/z (mod q) via 2-D lattice reduction / half-GCD and check [x]P − [z]Q = O (Antipa et al. 2005: >40% faster ECDSA verification; Pornin 2020: 30–33%; in SNARK circuits "fake GLV"/El Housni et al. 2025: 42–48% fewer constraints for [k]P, ≈34% for a full ECDSA verification); on secp256k1 the GLV endomorphism composes with this to give four ≈64-bit scalars. Without a hint, no technique verifying Q = [k]P in fewer group operations than computing it was found, and no useful random self-reduction exists in the literature surveyed.

### Cited Findings
- Antipa et al./Struik (Certicom, SAC 2005): ECDSA check Δ := (e s^{-1})G + (r s^{-1})Q − R = O is equivalent to μΔ = (μ e s^{-1})G + λQ − μR = O for any μ; choose r/s ≡ λ/μ (mod n) with λ, μ of half the bit-length of n via the extended Euclidean algorithm, and split μ e s^{-1} ≡ α0 + α1 t with G1 := tG precomputed; "Cost: half-size combination of known points G0, G1 and unknown points Q, R"; "Speed-up due to getting rid of half of so-called point doubles"; NIST prime / Suite B / Brainpool / NIST binary curves: 40% speed-up; requires knowing R (ECDSA*, or 1 side-information bit, or an agreed admissible-R convention; otherwise "roughly 8% average speed-up"); combined with key computation "ECDSA: up to 2.4x speed-up incremental ECDSA cost"; generalises to "Batch verification of multiple elliptic curve equations" — [Struik slides](https://av.fields.utoronto.ca/programs/scientific/07-08/cryptography/rene_struik.pdf); [DBLP](https://dblp.org/rec/conf/sacrypt/AntipaBGLSV05)
- Pornin, "Optimized Lattice Basis Reduction In Dimension 2, and Fast Schnorr and EdDSA Signature Verification", ePrint 2020/454: optimised Lagrange reduction makes "the Antipa et al. speed-up for ECDSA and EC Schnorr signatures worthwhile, even on very fast curves such as Ed25519"; verification time reduced by 30–33% on ARM Cortex-M0+/M4 and Intel Coffee Lake (measured on Curve9767) — [ePrint 2020/454](https://eprint.iacr.org/2020/454)
- In-circuit hinted scalar multiplication ("fake GLV", El Housni, ethresear.ch 2024): prover supplies Q = [s]P and half-size (u, v) with v·s ≡ u (mod r), u, v ≤ √r from a half-GCD; circuit checks [u]P − [v]Q = O (sign bit for negative v); gnark results on P-256 over BN254: [s]P 738,031 → 385,412 PLONKish constraints (−47.8%), 186,466 → 100,914 R1CS (−45.9%); full ECDSA verify 1,135,876 → 742,541 scs (−34.6%), 293,814 → 195,266 R1CS (−33.5%); P-384 and STARK-curve ≈−46% / −35%; 4-dimensional variant (GLV + fake GLV, Liam Eagen's idea) gives [u0]P + [u1]φ(P) − [v0]Q − [v1]φ(Q) = O with ≈r^{1/4} scalars for j=0 and j=1728 curves — [ethresear.ch Fake GLV](https://ethresear.ch/t/fake-glv-you-dont-need-an-efficient-endomorphism-to-implement-glv-like-scalar-multiplication-in-snark-circuits/20394)
- Eagen, El Housni, Masson, Piellard, "Fast Elliptic Curve Scalar Multiplications in SN(T)ARK Circuits", LATINCRYPT 2025 (ePrint 2025/933): lattice reduction / half-xGCD decomposition k = x/z mod r, CM-discriminant optimisations, generalisation to multi-scalar multiplication "as used in signature verification"; proving-time speed-up 22–53% vs prior state of the art; slide table (gnark): secp256k1 [k]P 385,461 → 223,188 PLONKish scs (−42%), 78,940 → 60,089 R1CS (−24%); BN254 381,467 → 220,436 scs (−42%); P-256 612,759 → 294,128 scs (−52%), 157,685 → 78,940 R1CS (−50%); with GLV and hint the check becomes a "Quadruple 64-bit scalar multiplication" ([x]P + [y]ψ(P) − [z]Q − [t]ψ(Q) = 0) and for a 2-MSM a "Sextuple 86-bit scalar multiplication"; "The scalar decomposition is not optimal yet (xgcd vs lll)" — [ePrint 2025/933](https://eprint.iacr.org/2025/933); [Latincrypt 2025 slides](https://yelhousni.github.io/latincrypt25.pdf); [talk page](https://yelhousni.github.io/talk/latincrypt25)
- Security caveat for hinted gadgets: El Housni, "Cofactor-torsion attacks on hinted scalar multiplications in SNARK circuits", ePrint 2026/1776: on curves with nontrivial cofactor the identity is checked in the full group and accepts Q' = [k]P + T for small torsion T; validated on BLS12-381, BN254, BW6-761; fix is a subgroup check or a cheaper "hinted preimage" binding — [ePrint 2026/1776](https://eprint.iacr.org/2026/1776)
- GLV on secp256k1 halves native verification cost ("This speed up cut the cost by 50%") and is unavailable on secp256r1 — [EIP-7212 discussion](https://ethereum-magicians.org/t/eip-7212-precompiled-for-secp256r1-curve-support/14789?page=3)

### Inferences
- For ecrecover inside a proof the natural statement is already "hinted": the prover knows Q, so instead of computing Q = r^{-1}(sR − zG) (inversion + 2 scalar mults + addition) the circuit checks sR − zG − rQ = O. Applying Antipa/fake-GLV: scale by μ/s with r/s ≡ λ/μ to get μR − λQ − (μ z/s)G = O, i.e. two ≈128-bit variable-base scalars (Straus-merged) plus a fixed-base term; with the secp256k1 endomorphism (j = 0, so the 4-D variant applies) the variable-base part becomes four ≈64-bit scalars on R, ψ(R), Q, ψ(Q) — ≈64 shared doublings + ≈4·64/(w+1) additions, versus ≈256 doublings + ≈2·256/(w+1) additions for the direct Straus check: roughly a 3x reduction in group operations for a single hinted verification, consistent with the 42–52% constraint savings measured for one [k]P and the ≈35% for full ECDSA (which still carries the fixed-base term and field inversions).
- secp256k1 has cofactor 1, so the cofactor-torsion attacks of ePrint 2026/1776 do not apply provided R and Q are checked to be on the curve (my inference from the paper's mechanism; the paper does not mention secp256k1).
- Hinted single verification (≈3x) and batching (≈4–9x at n = 100–10,000, KQ1) attack different costs and compose: in a batched MSM the Antipa trick is subsumed (every equation's scalars are already arbitrary), so the half-size decomposition only matters when signatures are verified one at a time or in very small batches.
- Without a hint there is no known way to verify a single Q = [k]P in fewer than ≈one scalar multiplication's worth of group operations: a random self-reduction (checking [k](P + S) for random S, etc.) still requires a full-size scalar multiplication, and the only sub-linear results in the literature are amortised across many instances (batching) or move work to an untrusted helper (hinting). This is an absence-of-evidence statement based on the sources surveyed.

### Gaps
- The Antipa et al. SAC 2005 paper text itself was not retrieved (Springer login); numbers are from Struik's slides and the abstract.
- No source measured hinted ECDSA/ecrecover verification specifically on secp256k1 with the 4-D (GLV + half-GCD) decomposition; the secp256k1 slide numbers are for a single [k]P.
- No published work on sub-linear single-scalar-multiplication verification without hints was found; "random self-reduction" style verification does not appear in the ECC literature surveyed (ExpSOS-style outsourcing uses blinding, not cheaper checking).
- Lookup-based amortisation of ECDSA (e.g. Lasso/Jolt-style tables for field arithmetic) was not found in any ECDSA-specific source; the only datapoint is that Jolt lacked the lookup support needed for ECDSA field arithmetic as of Aug 2025 (Fenbushi).
